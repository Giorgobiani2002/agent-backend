# ACCA F1 / FAB — Accountant in Business: Part A — The Business Organisation, Stakeholders and the External Environment

Source: BPP ACCA Approved Practice & Revision Kit, Foundations in Accountancy FAB / ACCA Paper F1, 5th edition January 2016, ISBN 9781472745927. Reproduced for internal accounting knowledge base.

## Core concepts

- Organisations can achieve results which individuals cannot achieve by themselves.
- A **non-governmental organisation (NGO)** is an independent voluntary association of people acting together for some common purpose (other than achieving government office or making money). NGOs are typically not commercial; they promote social, political or environmental change; they often raise funds through donations; they are measured by effectiveness and efficiency, and performance is often expressed as **Value For Money (VFM)** rather than ROCE or PBIT.
- **Stakeholders** are those individuals or groups that, potentially, have an interest in what the organisation does. They can be:
  - **Internal** (within the organisation, e.g. employees, managers, directors),
  - **Connected** (have a contractual relationship, e.g. shareholders, customers, suppliers, financiers),
  - **External** (e.g. government, regulators, pressure groups, local community, professional bodies).
- **Secondary stakeholders** are those who do not have a contractual relationship with the organisation; **primary stakeholders** do.

## Organisation types

- **Sole trader** — owner has unlimited personal liability for business debts.
- **Partnership** — ordinary partnerships do not have limited liability; partners are jointly liable.
- **Limited company** — ownership (shareholders) and control (directors) are legally separate. Shareholders' liability is limited to their unpaid share capital. Public limited companies (plc) can advertise shares to the general public; private companies (Ltd) cannot, but can raise share capital from venture capitalists.
- **Co-operative** — owned by members (workers or customers). Members share profits; each member has one vote regardless of contribution. Open membership, democratic control, distribution of surplus in proportion to purchases, promotion of education.
- **Public sector organisations** — owned and run by central or local government. Performance measured heavily by Value For Money (VFM) and efficiency indicators rather than profitability.
- **Voluntary sector organisations** — staffed largely by volunteers; charities are typical.

## Government policy and the macro environment

- **Government policy** influences the economic environment, the framework of laws, industry structure and certain operational issues. Political instability is a cause of risk.
- **Fiscal policy** is government policy on **taxation, government spending and government borrowing**. Exchange rates are NOT part of fiscal policy.
- **Monetary policy** is government policy on **interest rates, money supply, credit controls and exchange rates**.
- A **negative Public Sector Net Cash Requirement (PSNCR)** means the government is running a budget surplus — income exceeds expenditure, and Public Sector Debt Repayment (PSDR) is happening.
- **Direct taxes** are paid directly by a person/company to the Revenue authority (income tax, corporation tax, capital gains tax, inheritance tax).
- **Indirect taxes** are collected by the Revenue authority from a business, which attempts to pass on the tax to consumers in the price of goods (e.g. VAT/sales tax).
- **Progressive tax** — takes a higher proportion of income as income rises (e.g. income tax).
- **Regressive tax** — takes a higher proportion of income from low earners.
- **Proportional tax** — same proportion of income at all levels.

## Inflation and unemployment

- **Demand-pull inflation** arises from an excess of aggregate demand over the productive capacity of the economy.
- **Cost-push inflation** arises from increases in the costs of production.
- During high price inflation, debtors (those who owe money) gain — they pay back less in real terms. Creditors (those owed money) lose. Long-term liabilities are eroded.
- **Economic growth** may be measured by increases in the real gross national product (GNP) per head of the population.
- **Macroeconomic policy objectives**: economic growth, low and stable inflation, low unemployment, balance of payments equilibrium.
- **Stagflation** = low or negative economic growth combined with high inflation.
- **Frictional unemployment** is short-term and arises when there is difficulty matching workers quickly with jobs.
- **Structural unemployment** occurs when an industry declines and workers' skills cannot easily transfer to other industries in a reasonable geographic proximity (e.g. a shipbuilder made redundant when local yards close).
- **Cyclical unemployment** is caused by recession in the business cycle (e.g. lay-offs during a downturn in the building industry).
- **Seasonal unemployment** is regular and predictable (e.g. agricultural labourers in winter).
- **Technological unemployment** arises when automation replaces jobs.
- Governments can encourage **labour mobility** to reduce unemployment by offering relocation assistance and improving information on vacancies.

## International trade

- **Protectionist measures**: import quotas, tariffs (taxes on imports), customs procedures, subsidies for domestic producers. Subsidies for exporters are NOT protectionist — they promote exports rather than protect domestic producers from competition.
- A **balance of payments surplus or deficit** usually refers to a surplus/deficit on the **current account**.
- An **increase in a country's exchange rate** makes its exports more expensive abroad, makes imports cheaper, reduces demand for imports, and reduces cost-push inflation (because imports are cheaper).

## Demand, supply and elasticity

- In a **free market economy**, the price mechanism **allocates resources**.
- The **supply curve** of a firm in a competitive market is its **marginal cost curve above the average variable cost curve**. Below AVC, the firm shuts down.
- A **legal minimum price** below the equilibrium price has no effect; above equilibrium it creates excess supply (surplus).
- **Shifts in the demand curve** are caused by: changes in price of substitutes/complements, changes in income, advertising, changes in tastes, expectations of future price changes, demographic changes. A change in the price of the good itself causes a **movement along** the demand curve, not a shift.
- **Shifts in the supply curve** are caused by changes in production costs, technology, taxes/subsidies, expectations. A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the supply curve.
- **Substitutes** have positive cross-elasticity of demand (XED > 0): rise in price of one increases demand for the other.
- **Complements** have negative XED: rise in price of one decreases demand for the other (e.g. fish and chips, cars and petrol, cars and tyres).
- **Normal goods** have positive income elasticity of demand (IED > 0): demand rises as income rises.
- **Inferior goods** have negative IED: demand falls as income rises (e.g. starter homes, basic-quality goods).
- **Goods of ostentation** can have positive price elasticity of demand (PED > 0): higher price increases demand because of status signalling.
- **Price elasticity of demand (PED)** = % change in quantity / % change in price.
  - PED > 1 (absolute value): demand is elastic.
  - PED < 1: demand is inelastic.
  - PED = 1: unit elasticity.
- **Consumer surplus** = the excess between what consumers are willing to pay and the prevailing market price.
- **Utility maximisation rule**: marginal utility per dollar should be equal across all goods. MU(A)/P(A) = MU(B)/P(B) = ... within the consumer's budget.

## Market structures

- **Perfect competition**: many buyers and sellers, homogeneous product, no barriers to entry/exit, perfect information. The firm's demand curve is horizontal (perfectly elastic) — it can sell as much as it wants at the prevailing market price and nothing at all above it.
- **Monopoly**: one producer dominates the market.
- **Oligopoly**: a small number of large producers exert considerable influence in the market.
- **Monopolistic competition**: many producers, each differentiating their product (e.g. soft drinks).

## PEST and the external environment

- **PEST** = Political, Economic, Socio-cultural, Technological — the framework for analysing an organisation's external (macro) environment.
- **SWOT** = Strengths, Weaknesses (internal), Opportunities, Threats (external) — used in corporate appraisal, incorporating PEST.
- **SMART** = Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant, Time-bounded — characteristics of effective objectives. "M" stands for Measurable.
- **Demographics** = analysis of statistics on birth/death rates, age structures, ethnic groups, social class, etc. Birth and mortality rates directly affect the average age of a population: falling birth rate + falling death rate = ageing population.
- **Social class** for demographic purposes is identified by **income level, occupation, education** — NOT lifestyle.
- **Lifestyle** is generally a reflection of economic status, not a determinant of it.
- **Hawthorne Studies** (Elton Mayo) demonstrated that individual and group behaviour at work is a major factor in productivity, giving rise to the **human relations school** of management.

## Porter's Five Forces

The competitive environment of an industry is structured by five forces:
1. Threat of new entrants (barriers to entry).
2. Threat of substitute products or services.
3. Bargaining power of customers.
4. Bargaining power of suppliers.
5. Competitive rivalry among existing competitors.

Government regulation is NOT one of Porter's five forces (though it affects them).

**Barriers to entry** include: scale economies available to existing competitors, high capital investment requirements, strong brand loyalty, access to distribution channels, patents/regulations. **Low switching costs** are NOT a barrier to entry — they make it easier for customers to switch to a new entrant.

**Suppliers have high bargaining power** when: there is a lack of substitute products, the product is highly differentiated, switching costs for customers are high, suppliers are few, product quality is important to customers.

## Porter's Value Chain

**Primary activities** add value directly to the product/service:
- Inbound logistics (receiving and storing inputs).
- Operations (transforming inputs into outputs).
- Outbound logistics (storing and delivering outputs).
- Marketing and sales.
- After-sales service.

**Support activities** support the primary activities:
- Procurement.
- Technology development.
- Human resource management.
- Firm infrastructure (administration, legal, finance).

## Organisational change strategies

- **Downsizing** = reducing staff numbers throughout the organisation.
- **Delayering** = removing levels of (middle) management — often enabled by IT systems that connect strategic apex directly to operational core.
- **Outsourcing** = contracting out specified operations or services to an external vendor.
- **Delegation** = a superior gives a subordinate part of his/her own authority to make decisions. Responsibility cannot be delegated — the superior remains responsible.

## Marketing orientations

- **Production orientation** — assumes customers will buy whatever is produced; focus on efficient production.
- **Product orientation** — focus on adding features without market research.
- **Sales orientation** — focus on persuading customers to buy what the firm produces.
- **Marketing orientation** — focus on identifying and meeting customer needs; the firm researches what customers value and adapts products accordingly. This is the modern best-practice orientation.

The **four Ps** of the marketing mix: **Product, Price, Promotion, Place** (distribution). In services marketing, three more Ps are sometimes added: **People, Process, Physical evidence**.

**Market segmentation** = breaking up the market into different groups that share common needs, wants and preferences. Opposite of undifferentiated/mass marketing.

## Service characteristics

Services differ from products in four ways:
- **Intangibility** — no physical substance. Physical elements (tickets, vouchers, merchandise, confirmations) help to make services tangible to the customer.
- **Inseparability** — produced and consumed at the same time; cannot be stored.
- **Variability** — each instance of service is unique; standardisation is difficult.
- **Perishability** — services cannot be stored for later use.

## Employment law and discrimination

- **Unfair dismissal** is automatically considered to occur on grounds of: pregnancy, trade union membership/activity, race/sex/age/disability/religion discrimination, selection for redundancy based on protected characteristics.
- **Wrongful dismissal** refers to dismissal in breach of contract (e.g. without proper notice). It is distinct from unfair dismissal.
- **Redundancy** is potentially fair grounds for dismissal if the basis of selection is fair. Redundancy can occur when an employer ceases business in a particular location. In many jurisdictions, an employee with 2+ years' continuous employment is entitled to redundancy compensation.
- **Direct discrimination** = less favourable treatment of a protected group (e.g. setting age limits in a job advert, paying women less than men for the same job).
- **Indirect discrimination** = applying a requirement that disproportionately disadvantages a protected group and cannot be justified on non-discriminatory grounds (e.g. requiring minimum height that excludes most women; offering less favourable terms to part-time workers when most are women).
- **Harassment** = use of threatening, intimidating, offensive or abusive language or behaviour focused on a protected characteristic.
- **Victimisation** = penalising someone because they have made a claim of discrimination or supported one.
- **Positive action** = encouraging applications from disadvantaged groups, providing targeted training. Legal.
- **Positive discrimination** = giving preference to protected groups regardless of merit (e.g. selection quotas based on ethnicity rather than ability). Generally illegal in most jurisdictions.
- **Equal pay** law requires women receive equal pay to men for **work of equal value** — not necessarily the same job. Job evaluation is the recommended means of establishing relative value, but is not compulsory.
- **Disability discrimination law** requires employers to make **reasonable adjustments** to working arrangements or premises to remove disadvantage to disabled people. The qualifying threshold is usually an impairment with adverse effect of more than 12 months' duration.

## Managing diversity vs equal opportunities

- **Equal opportunities** is a narrower concept based on non-discrimination on crude protected characteristics (sex, race, age, etc.).
- **Managing diversity** is broader — based on the belief that organisations benefit from embracing the full range of individual differences (personality, working style, goals, etc.) within their workforce, reflecting the diversity of their customer and labour markets.

## Health and safety

- The new recruit must receive the health & safety policy statement **as early as possible after employment** — ideally on day one.
- Employers' duties include: providing safe systems of work, competent safety advisers, training, controls to reduce risks, communication of the safety policy.
- Employees' duties include: taking reasonable care of themselves, finding out about the safety policy, co-operating with the employer's measures.
- An employee being aware of a risk does NOT mean they consent to it — the employer remains responsible for a safe working system.
