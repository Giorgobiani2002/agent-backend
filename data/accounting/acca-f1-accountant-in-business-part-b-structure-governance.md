# ACCA F1 / FAB — Accountant in Business: Part B — Business Organisation Structure, Functions and Governance

Source: BPP ACCA Approved Practice & Revision Kit, FAB / ACCA Paper F1, 5th edition January 2016.

## Mintzberg's components of organisation

Henry Mintzberg classified five basic components of an organisation:
1. **Strategic apex** — top management; ensures the organisation pursues its objectives and serves the needs of owners and stakeholders. Exerts a pull to centralise, leading to the simple structure.
2. **Middle line** — middle managers who organise, plan and control work; act as an interface between the strategic apex and the operating core.
3. **Operating core** — non-managerial workers who secure inputs, process them and distribute outputs (e.g. production workers).
4. **Technostructure** — analysts (accountants, work-planners, IT specialists) whose objective is to standardise work through systems, schedules, procedures and budgets.
5. **Support staff** — provide ancillary services (canteen, legal, HR, mailroom).

## Organisational structure types

- **Functional departmentation** — grouped by specialism (finance, marketing, production). Good for recruiting technical specialists; can create silos.
- **Geographical departmentation** — grouped by region. Decisions can be made at a local level rather than centrally.
- **Product/brand departmentation** — grouped by product line.
- **Divisional structure** — semi-autonomous business units, often by product or region. Suits diversified, growing companies (e.g. after acquisitions).
- **Matrix structure** — dual reporting (e.g. to a functional manager and a project/product manager). Challenges the classical principle of **unity of command** ("one person, one boss"). Advantages: flexibility, improved communications, employee motivation. Disadvantage: dual authority can cause conflict.
- **Hybrid structures** — combine multiple types.
- **Boundaryless organisation** — removes internal hierarchical barriers and external barriers between the organisation and suppliers/customers/competitors.
- **Hollow organisation** — splits activities into core (retained in-house) and non-core (outsourced).
- **Modular organisation** — different components of the product/service are outsourced to different suppliers.
- **Jobless structure** — the employee is a seller of skills rather than a job-holder.
- **Virtual organisation** — exists primarily electronically; geographically dispersed teams connected via ICT.
- **Shared service centre** — a single internal unit serves multiple parts of the organisation (commonly IT, HR, finance). Services are likely to be less tailored; the centre IS part of the organisation, though its relationship resembles that of an external service provider.

## Span of control and structure shape

- **Span of control** = the number of subordinates directly responsible to a superior. NOT the number of all subordinates in the hierarchy below.
- **Narrow span** is appropriate where: work is complex; team members' work differs significantly; team is inexperienced; team is geographically dispersed.
- **Wide span** is appropriate where: work is routine; team is experienced; subordinates' work is similar.
- Narrow span → tall organisation (many levels); wide span → flat organisation (few levels).
- Recent trends are towards **delayering** — removing levels of (middle) management.
- A 9.1 leadership style on the Blake & Mouton grid is highly task-focused with low concern for people.

## Centralisation vs decentralisation

**Advantages of centralisation**:
- Senior managers can take a wider, organisation-wide view.
- Procedures and documentation can be standardised.
- Consistency of decision-making across the organisation.
- Avoids local managers making decisions that conflict with overall strategy.

**Advantages of decentralisation**:
- Decision makers have greater awareness of local conditions and problems.
- Reduces bureaucracy.
- Helps develop the skills and motivation of junior managers.
- More accountability at lower levels.
- Better crisis management.
- Avoids overburdening top managers.

## Informal organisation

- An **informal organisation** always exists alongside the formal one.
- The Hawthorne Studies (Mayo) highlighted the influence of informal groups on productivity, attitudes and norms.
- Informal organisation can: stimulate innovation; pose threats to health & safety (via short-cuts); facilitate communication (the "grapevine"); bypass secretive management; reinforce or undermine the formal system.
- Managers in positions of authority CAN be part of informal organisation; they can also feed information into the grapevine.
- The grapevine is fast, selective, up-to-date, but NOT always accurate.

## Handy's four cultural types (after Harrison)

Each type associated with a Greek god:
- **Power culture (Zeus)** — shaped by one individual, often the founder. Simple structure; informal. Suits small, entrepreneurial firms.
- **Role culture (Apollo)** — bureaucratic, shaped by rationality, rules and procedures. Suits stable environments, large established organisations. Specialisation, scalar chain of command, unity of command, vertical communication.
- **Task culture (Athena)** — shaped by focus on outputs, results and problem-solving. Suits matrix structures, project work, dynamic environments (e.g. consultancies). Highly flexible and reactive.
- **Existential / person culture (Dionysus)** — shaped by interests of individuals. Suits partnerships of professionals (e.g. barristers, doctors, academics).

## Schein's three levels of culture

1. **Observable / artefacts** — concrete expressions: dress code, office design, ceremonies, symbols, behaviour, patterns of greeting.
2. **Values and beliefs** — consciously held; give meaning to artefacts.
3. **Underlying assumptions** — deepest level; taken for granted; hardest to manage and change. These "programme" how the culture perceives, thinks and reacts.

Schein definition: "the set of shared, taken-for-granted implicit assumptions that a group holds and that determines how it perceives, thinks about and reacts to its environment".

## Hofstede's cultural dimensions

Geert Hofstede classified four (later more) dimensions of national/organisational cultural difference:
1. **Power-distance**:
   - **High power-distance** — accepts greater centralisation, top-down chain of command, closer supervision. Hierarchical.
   - **Low power-distance** — flat structures, high employee participation in decision-making, delegation, less formal authority differential.
2. **Uncertainty avoidance**:
   - **High uncertainty avoidance** — strong task structure, written rules, intolerance of dissent, risk-averse, dissent not tolerated.
   - **Low uncertainty avoidance** — values flexibility and creativity, less task structure, tolerance of risk, dissent, conflict, deviation from norms.
3. **Individualism vs collectivism**:
   - **High individualism** — emphasis on autonomy, task achievement over relationships, impersonal organisations defending business interests.
   - **Low individualism (collectivism)** — emphasis on interdependence, reciprocal obligation, social acceptability; organisation seen as a family.
4. **Masculinity vs femininity**:
   - **High masculinity** — assertiveness, competition, decisiveness, material success; clear gender role differentiation.
   - **Low masculinity (femininity)** — relationships, quality of life, modesty, caring; acceptability of these values for both genders. A nurturing manager who shelters staff from compulsory redundancies via consultation and counselling shows low-masculinity values.

Culture definition (Hofstede): "the collective programming of the mind which distinguishes the members of one category of people from another". Culture applies to nations, organisations, social classes, ethnic groups, genders etc.

## Functions of management

**Fayol's five functions of management**: **Planning, Organising, Commanding, Co-ordinating, Controlling**.
(Motivating is NOT one of Fayol's five — it was added by later writers who replaced "commanding" with consultative functions.)

**Drucker's five categories of management work**:
1. Setting objectives.
2. Organising the work.
3. Motivating and communicating.
4. The job of measurement.
5. Developing people.
("Planning and control" is not a separate category — they fall within setting objectives and measurement.)

**Mintzberg's nine managerial roles** in three categories:
- **Interpersonal**: Figurehead, Leader, Liaison.
- **Informational**: Monitor (gathers information from internal and external contacts), Disseminator (spreads relevant info to team), Spokesperson (provides info on behalf of the organisation).
- **Decisional**: Entrepreneur, Disturbance handler, Resource allocator, Negotiator.

## Power, authority, influence and responsibility

- **Authority** = the right to perform an action.
- **Power** = the ability to perform an action (and influence others).
- **Responsibility** = the obligation/duty to perform an action. Cannot be delegated.
- **Influence** = the process of modifying another's behaviour.
- **Position (legitimate) power** — by virtue of formal organisational role.
- **Expert power** — by virtue of valued knowledge.
- **Personal (referent/charisma) power** — by force of personality.
- **Coercive power** — backed by physical force or punishment.
- **Reward power** — control over reward distribution.
- **Resource power** — control over scarce resources.
- **Negative power** — power to disrupt or stop things happening (e.g. a refused request, a work-to-rule).

Leaders may be distinguished from managers by their independence from **position power** — they exercise informal, interpersonal forms of influence such as personal and expert power.

## Schools of leadership theory

1. **Trait ("qualities") theories** — leaders are born with certain qualities.
2. **Style theories** — focus on what leaders do (their behaviour):
   - **Ashridge model**: tells (autocratic), sells (persuasive), consults (consultative), joins (democratic). Consultative is most popular among subordinates; "sells" is most popular among leaders; "tells" can drive highest productivity in certain conditions.
   - **Blake & Mouton managerial grid**: two axes — concern for production (task) and concern for people, each scored 1–9. Key positions: 1.1 impoverished (low both); 1.9 country club (high people, low task); 9.1 task management / authority-compliance (high task, low people — pushes for results regardless of team feelings); 5.5 dampened pendulum / middle of the road; 9.9 team management (high both — ideal).
   - **Likert's four styles**: exploitative authoritative, benevolent authoritative, consultative, participative (democratic).
3. **Contingency theories** — there is no "one best way"; effective style depends on the situation:
   - **Fiedler** — distinguishes psychologically distant managers (PDM: task-oriented, prefer formal procedures, judge by performance, maintain distance from staff) vs psychologically close managers (PCM: closer to staff, prefer informal contact).
   - **John Adair's action-centred leadership** — balances three interlocking circles: task needs, individual needs, group needs. Within contingency school but focuses on what leaders do.
   - **Hersey & Blanchard situational leadership** — leadership style varies with the maturity (ability/willingness) of the followers.
   - **Tannenbaum & Schmidt continuum** — autocratic to democratic.

**Heifetz** — theory of **dispersed leadership** — individuals at any level can exert leadership influence.

**Bennis** — articulated key differences between **managers** and **leaders**:
- Manager administers; leader innovates.
- Manager focuses on systems and structure; leader focuses on people.
- Manager does things right; leader does the right thing.

## Marketing and the marketing function

- The marketing function manages the organisation's relationships with its customers.
- The **main purpose of marketing** is to **identify and anticipate customer needs**.
- Marketing function provides information relevant to all elements of the marketing mix. Information from the **procurement (purchasing) manager** about input costs is most relevant to the **Price** element of the mix.

## Research and Development (R&D)

- R&D is concerned primarily with **product research** (not market research).
- R&D may improve existing products as well as developing new ones.
- R&D is critically important in industries with short product life cycles (e.g. next-generation computers); less critical when manufacturing to an industry standard.
- R&D can be conducted internally or contracted to external specialists. Many organisations have internal R&D departments.

## Human Resource Management (HRM)

- HRM is concerned with the most effective use of human resources: organisation design, staffing levels, motivation, employee relations, employee services.
- HRM does NOT manage the organisation's relationship with customers (that is marketing).
- Current trend is to **devolve recruitment and selection** (and other HR activities) **to line managers** rather than centralising them within HR.

## Committees

- **Standing committee** — formed for a particular purpose on a permanent basis (e.g. monthly staff committee, audit committee).
- **Joint committee** — co-ordinates activities of two or more committees.
- **Task force / ad hoc committee** — formed for a specific task; winds up when complete.
- **Sub-committee** — created by a parent committee to handle specific delegated business.

**Committee Chair** is responsible for: fixing date, time, location; calling meetings; ensuring agenda is followed; giving rulings on procedural disputes; ensuring fair participation.

**Committee Secretary** is responsible for: preparing and issuing documents (agenda, papers) before the meeting; taking minutes during the meeting; communicating decisions and following up actions afterwards.

**Agenda order** (typical formal meeting):
1. Apologies for absence.
2. Approval of minutes of previous meeting.
3. Matters arising out of those minutes.
4. Subjects for discussion (main business).
5. Any other business (AOB).
6. Date of next meeting.

**Disadvantages of committees**: time-consuming, expensive, invite compromise rather than clear-cut decisions, dilute accountability, may be ineffective without strong chairing.

## Corporate governance

- **Corporate governance** is the system by which organisations are directed and controlled by their senior officers. It is essentially of **strategic importance**.
- Good corporate governance involves: risk management, internal control, accountability to stakeholders, ethical and effective conduct of business.
- The **board of directors** has ultimate responsibility for good corporate governance. The board's role (per the King report) is to:
  1. Define the **purpose** of the company.
  2. Define the **values** by which the company will perform its daily duties.
  3. Identify the **stakeholders** relevant to the company.
  4. Develop a **strategy** combining these factors.
  5. Ensure **implementation** of the strategy.

**Features of poor corporate governance**:
- Domination of the board by a single individual (e.g. combined Chair and CEO).
- Lack of independent scrutiny.
- Lack of supervision of staff in key roles.
- Excessive focus on short-term profitability that may cause manipulation of results.

**Division of responsibilities** at the head of the organisation is most simply achieved by **separating the roles of Chair and Chief Executive**.

**Non-executive directors** (NEDs) provide:
- Wider perspective than executive directors.
- External experience and knowledge.
- Reassurance to shareholders.
- Independent oversight.
Disadvantages: limited time, may not know the company in depth.

**Audit committee** — composed of independent non-executive directors. Responsibilities:
- Liaise with the external auditor.
- Supervise internal audit.
- Review the annual accounts and internal controls.
- Review the organisation's risk management.
The audit committee should NOT act as a barrier between the executive board and external auditors — the board should remain open to auditor questioning.

**Nomination committee** — oversees the process for board appointments.

**Remuneration committee** — composed of independent non-executive directors. Sets directors' reward and incentive packages. Should NOT be the executive board itself (otherwise directors set their own pay).

**Public Oversight Board** — monitors and enforces legal and compliance standards.

**Annual reports** must convey a **fair and balanced view** of the organisation. Should state compliance with governance codes and give specific disclosures on: board composition, internal control review, going concern status, relations with stakeholders.

**Operating and Financial Review (OFR)** — sets out the directors' analysis of the business, providing investors with a historical and prospective view through the eyes of management.

## Corporate governance theories

- **Agency theory** — managers (agents) will only act in shareholders' interests if they have incentives to do so; hence performance-related pay and bonus schemes.
- **Stewardship theory** — managers are stewards of the assets of the firm and naturally act in shareholders' interests.
- **Stakeholder theory** — managers have a duty of care to a range of organisational stakeholders, not only shareholders.

## Codes of practice on corporate governance

In most countries, codes of practice on corporate governance provide **guidance on the standards of best practice that companies should adopt** — they are typically principles-based rather than legally binding rules.

Examples of regulation strength (least to most):
- Corporate Social Responsibility — least regulated; mostly voluntary and discretionary.
- Corporate governance — codes of best practice plus some law.
- Employment protection — most regulated by law.

## Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)

- CSR principles include: human rights, ethical employment, employee welfare, sustainability, support for local suppliers, environmental responsibility.
- Professional ethics is at the individual level; CSR operates at the firm level.
- CSR is NOT confined to businesses — charities, governments and other organisations also have social responsibilities.
- Social responsibility may have commercial benefits (reputation, customer loyalty, employee engagement) but does NOT guarantee profit. In the short term, CSR action may adversely affect shareholders' returns; in the long term it tends to align with sustainable performance.
- **Environmental audit** monitors compliance with environmental regulations, emissions levels and the organisation's environmental impact.
- Guaranteeing a "living wage" is an example of CSR.
- **Sustainability** can involve: using local suppliers, minimising energy consumption, long-term supplier relationships, ethical employment, reducing waste.

## Stakeholder analysis — Mendelow's matrix

Stakeholders are mapped on two axes: **Power** (low/high) and **Level of Interest** (low/high), producing four quadrants:
- Low power, low interest — **minimal effort**.
- Low power, high interest — **keep informed**.
- High power, low interest — **keep satisfied**.
- High power, high interest — **key players** — actively manage and consult.

Local residents in a building dispute often start with high interest but low power; if local politicians join their committee, they become high-power, high-interest **key players**.
