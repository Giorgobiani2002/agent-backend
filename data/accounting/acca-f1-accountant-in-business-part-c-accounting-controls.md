# ACCA F1 / FAB — Accountant in Business: Part C — Accounting and Reporting Systems, Controls and Compliance

Source: BPP ACCA Approved Practice & Revision Kit, FAB / ACCA Paper F1, 5th edition January 2016.

## The role of accounting

- **Accounting** is a way of recording, analysing and summarising the transactions of a business.
- The main aim of accounting is **to provide financial information to users**.
- Different users have different reasons for wanting financial information about a large public company:
  - **Shareholders** — to assess how effectively management is running the company (the stewardship function).
  - **Suppliers** — to assess the ability of the company to pay its debts.
  - **Tax authorities** — to assess tax payable.
  - **Financial analysts** — to advise clients on investment decisions.
  - **Employees** — to assess job security and attainment of company objectives.
  - **Public / local community** — to evaluate social impacts and contributions.
  - **Customers** — to assess continuity of supply.
  - **Government** — to monitor compliance and economic activity.
  - **Lenders / financiers** — to assess ability to maintain and repay loans.
- Charities, public bodies and professional bodies also produce financial statements, though for different purposes (accountability to trustees, members or funding bodies).

## Key financial statements

- **Statement of profit or loss (SOPL)** — records income generated and expenditure incurred over a given period. Operating costs as a percentage of turnover are read from the SOPL.
- **Statement of financial position (SOFP / balance sheet)** — list of all assets owned and liabilities owed at a particular date. Equity (shareholders' funds), long-term borrowings and liquidity (current assets vs current liabilities) are read from the SOFP. Company law requires it to give a **true and fair view** of the company's state of affairs at the year-end.
- **Statement of cash flows** — shows sources of cash generated during a period and how those funds have been spent.
- **Audit report** — separate document expressing the external auditor's opinion on whether the financial statements give a true and fair view.

## Information levels

- **Strategic information** — long-term, used by senior management. Example: 5-year cash forecasts; product development plans for the next 2–3 years.
- **Tactical (management) information** — medium-term, used by middle management. Examples: annual revenue/cost forecasts for a department; targets agreed at performance appraisals; departmental budgets.
- **Operational information** — short-term, day-to-day, used by supervisors and operatives.

## Qualities of good information (ACCURATE)

Information should be:
- **A**ccurate (correct enough for the purpose).
- **C**omplete.
- **C**ost-beneficial (worth more than it costs to produce).
- **U**nderstandable / comprehensible to the user.
- **R**elevant (information not needed for a decision should be omitted, however interesting).
- **A**uthoritative (from a reliable source).
- **T**imely.
- **E**asy to use.

Information should also be **objective**, **reliable** and **comparable** across periods.

**Data** is unprocessed input. **Information** is data processed and presented in a way meaningful to the receiver. Data is not necessarily numerical; information is not necessarily textual.

Examples:
- Completed questionnaires from market research → **data**.
- Variance reports for a department manager → **information**.
- An individual employee's test score → **data**.
- Average employee assessment grade for a company → **information**.
- Calculations showing whether an investment is profitable → **information**.
- Company website page → **information**.

## The finance function — roles

- **Financial Controller** — routine accounting, accounting reports, cashier's duties, cash control. Often responsible for the **financial accounting** function: payroll, receivables and payables ledger, credit control, financial accounts, taxation, statutory accounts. Day-to-day operations and statutory reporting.
- **Management Accountant** — provides information for management: cost accounting, budgets and budgetary control, financial management of projects, inventory valuation, job costing, variance analysis, planning.
- **Treasurer** — treasury management: raising and investing funds, cash flow control, foreign currency exposure, repaying loans, arranging overdrafts. Responsible for capital appraisal and project investment decisions where there is no separate Financial Manager.
- **Financial Manager** — also decides how much credit should be given to customers.

**Treasury responsibilities**:
- Arranging an overdraft.
- Managing foreign currency exposure.
- Cash budgeting / cash flow control.
- Repaying loans.

**Financial accountant responsibilities**:
- Recording financial transactions.
- Reporting to shareholders.
- Statutory accounts and tax returns.
- Payroll and ledger management.

## Markets for funds

- **Money markets** — markets for trading short-term financial instruments such as certificates of deposit, treasury bills, commercial paper.
- **Capital markets** — markets for trading long-term securities (shares, bonds).
- **Venture capital** — provides equity to small/growing businesses, typically private.

## Company secretary role

The company secretary is responsible for:
- Establishment and maintenance of a registered office.
- Filing of annual returns and other statutory documents.
- Maintaining statutory books and records.
- Convening meetings (board meetings, AGMs) and giving notice.
- Recording minutes of meetings.

The company secretary does NOT chair management committee meetings (that is typically the CEO or chair).

## Accounting and reporting systems

- A **transaction processing system** records and summarises individual transactions (sales, purchases, payroll). Example: a system used by a company to summarise sales and purchases.
- A **management information system (MIS)** processes data into information for routine management decisions.
- A **decision support system (DSS)** supports semi-structured decision-making, often with what-if analysis.
- An **executive support system (ESS)** pools data from internal and external sources for strategic, unstructured decision-making by senior managers.
- An **expert system** draws on a knowledge base to solve well-defined technical problems.
- An **office automation system (OAS)** is primarily designed to increase the **productivity** of data and information workers (word processing, email, scheduling).

## Accounting software

- A **module** is a program that deals with one particular part of a computerised business accounting system (e.g. sales ledger module, payroll module).
- A **suite** is a set of integrated modules covering an accounting system.
- **Integrated accounting packages** offer advantages over stand-alone programs in: efficiency in updating data (one entry updates multiple records), flexibility in reporting, and data integrity (fewer inconsistencies). They may have fewer specialised capabilities than dedicated stand-alone programs.
- A **database** is a structured, centralised pool of data which can be accessed by a number of applications. Used for accounts, HR, customer records etc. A database requires updating as data changes.
- A **spreadsheet** is used to display and process numerical data; particularly useful for creating **financial models**, "what-if" scenarios, logical tests. Spreadsheets do NOT automatically correct data entered by the operator — garbage in, garbage out.

## Advantages of computerised accounting systems over manual systems

- Faster processing.
- Reduced risk of computation errors (though input errors remain — "garbage in, garbage out").
- Easier corrections.
- More accessible information (especially with integrated systems).
- Easier to update as new information becomes available.
- Better presentation options (graphs, charts, formats).

**Computerised systems do NOT automatically**:
- Reject inaccurate input (without specific validation controls).
- Provide data security (which depends on controls implemented).
- Eradicate all errors.

## Exception reporting

**Exception reporting** = focusing reports on those items where performance differs significantly from standard or budget. Helps managers focus on areas needing attention. It is an upward-communication aid AND a way to reduce information overload on senior managers.

## Regulatory framework

- **IASB** (International Accounting Standards Board) — issues **International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS)**. Its key objective is the development of a single set of enforceable global accounting standards. NOT issued by the European Union or ASB.
- **ASB** (Accounting Standards Board) — UK national standard-setter, issues Financial Reporting Standards (FRSs).
- **IFAC** (International Federation of Accountants) — international body representing major accountancy bodies. Its mission is to develop high standards of professional accountants and enhance the quality of services they provide. Promotes ethical standards (administered by the **IESBA** — International Ethics Standards Board for Accountants). IFAC has NO legal powers; it does NOT issue financial reporting standards.
- **GAAP** (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles) — local conventions of accepted accounting practice.
- **European Union** — promotes harmonisation across member states.

The preparation and filing of accounts by limited companies is required by **national legislation**, not by codes of corporate governance or accounting standards directly.

## Internal control

Internal controls aim to:
- Enable the organisation to respond appropriately to business, operational and financial risks.
- Help ensure the quality of internal and external reporting.
- Help ensure compliance with applicable laws and regulations.
- Provide **reasonable assurance** (not absolute) that the organisation will fulfil its objectives.

Internal controls do NOT eliminate the possibility of human error, deliberate override, poor judgement or unforeseen circumstances.

**Inherent limitations of internal control systems**:
- Cost of control (must not outweigh benefit).
- Human error and deliberate override.
- Types of transactions controls cover (routine vs non-routine).
- Collusion can defeat segregation of duties.

## Classifications of controls

- **Administrative controls** — reporting structures, communication channels, policies.
- **Accounting controls** — designed to safeguard assets and ensure reliability of records.
- **Prevent / detect / correct controls**:
  - **Prevent** — stop errors happening (e.g. password access, segregation of duties).
  - **Detect** — find errors after they happen (e.g. reconciliations, audit trails).
  - **Correct** — restore data or system after error (e.g. system recovery, restoring from back-ups).
- **Discretionary controls** — can be by-passed by human choice (e.g. authorisation of purchase, audit-trail review).
- **Non-discretionary controls** — built into the system; cannot be by-passed (e.g. balancing entries must balance; password protection that must be entered).
- **Voluntary controls** — chosen by the organisation.
- **Mandated controls** — required by law/regulation, imposed externally.
- **Manual vs automated controls**.

A control system consisting of sufficient controls is NOT necessarily fully effective — the controls must also be applied properly and honestly.

## SPAMSOAP — financial control procedures mnemonic

- **S** — Segregation of duties.
- **P** — Physical (locks, safes, secured premises, restricted access).
- **A** — Authorisation and approval.
- **M** — Management (supervision by management).
- **S** — Supervision (of operations).
- **O** — Organisation (clear reporting lines, authority, responsibility).
- **A** — Arithmetic and accounting (control totals, reconciliations).
- **P** — Personnel (recruitment, training, induction, discipline).

## Internal checks vs internal controls

**Internal checks** are checks on day-to-day transactions where the work of one person is proved independently or is complementary to another's:
- Pre-lists, post-lists and control totals.
- Bank reconciliations.
- Separation of duties for authorising, custody and recording.

Authorisation systems with spending limits are an internal **control**, not an internal check. The use of control totals is an example of an internal check.

## Control over cash receipts

Three main areas:
1. **Receipts must be banked promptly** to reduce risk of theft.
2. **The record of receipts must be complete** so all receipts are accounted for.
3. **The loss of receipts through theft or accident must be prevented** through physical security and documentation.

## Control over accounts payable / purchases

Aims include:
- Ensure all credit notes received are recorded in the general and payables ledger.
- Ensure all purchases are properly authorised.
- Ensure only goods/services actually received are paid for.
- Match purchase orders, goods received notes (GRN) and invoices before payment.

## Control over accounts receivable / sales

Aims include:
- Goods/services are supplied only to customers with good credit ratings.
- All credit notes issued are recorded in the general and receivables ledgers.
- Potentially doubtful debts are identified.

A **receivables (sales ledger) control account**:
- Provides a check on the arithmetical accuracy of the personal ledger.
- Helps to improve separation of duties.
- Can deter fraud.
- (Does NOT guarantee that there are no errors in the personal ledger — it only catches certain kinds of error.)

## Data security controls

- **Physical access controls**: door locks, intruder alarms, card entry systems, security guards. Card entry systems and PINs are physical/access controls.
- **Logical access controls**: passwords, encryption, biometrics. Password-only access protects from accidental damage and casual hackers.
- **Input controls**: check digits, control totals, limit checks, validation rules.
- **Processing controls**: batch totals, run-to-run reconciliations.
- **Output controls**: distribution lists, control of printouts.
- **Audit trails** — records showing who has accessed a system and what operations were performed. Enable security monitoring, transaction checking, fraud detection and recovery of lost transactions.
- **Back-up controls** — copies of data and software stored in separate locations. These are **correct** controls (recovery in case of failure), not access controls.
- **Contingency controls** — system recovery procedures activated in the event of breakdown or disaster. Help an organisation recover.
- **Security controls** — protect data from unauthorised modification, disclosure or destruction.
- **Uninterruptible (protected) power supplies** protect data and IT systems from **accidental damage** (power surges, lightning, blackouts).
- A computer system not connected to the Internet cannot receive email from external sources, but still needs back-up, password access and basic security.

Loss or corruption of data is NOT always non-deliberate — fraud, sabotage, malicious damage and commercial espionage are deliberate threats. New staff pose risks via inexperience and error. It is not always cost-effective to prevent all threats. Smoke detectors are a form of physical data protection (against fire).

## Internal audit vs external audit

| Aspect | Internal audit | External audit |
|---|---|---|
| Independence | Independent appraisal activity within the org; reports to the audit committee of the board | Independent of the company; appointed by shareholders |
| Purpose | Add value, improve operations, evaluate internal controls | Express an opinion on the financial statements (true and fair view) |
| Focus | Operations, systems, risk management | Financial statements and underlying records |
| Reports to | Audit committee / those charged with governance | Shareholders |
| Mandatory | Discretionary in most jurisdictions | Required by law for most companies |

**Internal audit is an independent appraisal activity** but is still part of the organisation's internal control system. It is a service to management.

**External auditors are appointed by shareholders** (not the chairman, finance director, or board). Their **primary responsibility** is to report to shareholders on whether the financial statements give a true and fair view — NOT to investigate financial irregularities. They may rely on the work of internal audit if they first assess its quality and independence.

## Types of audit

- **Systems audit** — testing and evaluating internal controls; the basis for compliance testing.
- **Operational / efficiency / management / value-for-money audit** — concerned with monitoring management's performance, the outputs of the system, and the efficiency of the organisation.
- **Transactions / probity audit** — checks account entries to identify errors or omissions, often via substantive tests; aims to detect fraud.
- **Social audit** — measures social responsibility or social impacts.

**Substantive tests** are designed to identify errors and omissions in financial records — they "substantiate" the figures.
**Compliance tests** are designed to establish whether internal controls are being applied as prescribed.

External audit responsibilities include:
- Reporting to shareholders that the financial accounts present a true and fair view.
- Confirming that the financial accounts comply with legal requirements.

External audit responsibilities do NOT include preventing fraud or ensuring policies are in place — those are management/director responsibilities.

## Reliance on internal audit by external auditors

External auditors may place reliance on internal auditors' work, but only after assessing:
- Internal audit's status, scope and independence.
- Technical competence of internal audit staff.
- Due professional care (planning, supervision, review, working papers).

Indicators that external auditor should NOT rely on internal audit work include: no audit manuals or working papers, lack of independence from management, lack of technical qualification of internal auditors.

Indicators in favour of reliance include: direct reporting line to the audit committee, evidence that management acts on recommendations, technical qualifications and demonstrated proficiency of staff.

## Fraud — definitions and pre-conditions

**Fraud** in a corporate context falls into two main categories:
1. **Removal of funds or assets** from a business (theft, embezzlement).
2. **Intentional misrepresentation of the financial position** of a business (manipulation of accounts, false reporting).

Three **pre-conditions** must exist for fraud (the "fraud triangle"):
- **Dishonesty** — a willingness or pre-disposition to act in ways that contravene accepted ethical, social, organisational or legal norms.
- **Motivation** — an incentive (financial pressure, greed, grievance, performance targets).
- **Opportunity** — an opening (weak controls, lack of segregation, complex transactions, computer access).

## Examples of fraud

- **Teeming and lading** — a method of fraud in accounts receivable: cash or cheque receipts are stolen, and the theft concealed by setting subsequent receipts against the outstanding debt of the first customer. Most likely in **sales** / cash-handling areas.
- **Payroll fraud** — fictitious staff ("ghost employees"), falsified time-sheets, unauthorised pay rate changes. Both insiders and outsiders can perpetrate.
- **Expense fraud** — fictitious expenses or inflated claims.
- **Manipulating depreciation figures** — easy to do because depreciation has no cash-flow effect; can mask declining asset values or boost reported profit.
- **Understating expenses** — inflates reported profit.
- **Channel-stuffing / fake sales** — selling goods to friends or related parties with secret promise to buy them back.
- **Asset misappropriation** — theft of inventory or cash.
- **Overstating financial position** — affects investors and suppliers most directly (false confidence in solvency, leading to credit and investment decisions on inaccurate information).
- **Understating results** — restricts access to loan finance and may understate distributable profits.

## Vulnerability factors

**Internal risk factors that increase fraud profile**:
- Corporate restructuring (disruption of control).
- New personnel (unknowns, weakened familiarity controls).
- Upgraded or new management information system (transition risk).
- High staff morale REDUCES risk; low morale and grievances INCREASE risk.
- Staff NOT taking their full holiday entitlement is a personnel risk indicator — they may be hiding fraud that would be uncovered during a temporary replacement.
- Performance-based rewards for managers can INCREASE risk by motivating manipulation of results.
- Autocratic management style or domination by a chief executive can reduce questioning.

**External factors**:
- Increased competition (puts pressure on margins).
- Economic downturn.
- Technology change.

## Computer fraud risks

Key risk areas:
- Hackers.
- Lack of managerial understanding of IT.
- Integration of data systems (frauds easier to conceal because consistency is automatic).
- Cash sales (high risk).

Securing access to data is NOT a "key risk area" — it is a control that mitigates risk. Modern systems offer many access controls (passwords, encryption, biometrics) — the issue is whether the organisation uses them.

## Fraud prevention measures

- **Segregation of duties** — separating authorisation, custody and recording.
- **Appropriate documentation** — purchase requisitions, orders, GRNs, invoices, with audit trail.
- **Limitation control** — restricting authority (e.g. spending limits; using only approved suppliers).
- **Authorisation policies** — sign-offs for transactions.
- **Physical security** — locks, alarms.
- **Sequential numbering** of transaction documents.
- **Recruitment controls** — references, vetting.
- **Fraud awareness training and ethics education**.
- **Disciplinary codes**.
- **Internal checks and controls**.
- **Rotation of duties** and mandatory holiday.

Match fraud pre-conditions to control strategies:
- **Motivation** → disciplinary codes and sanctions to make fraud unattractive.
- **Opportunity** → internal checks and controls to close openings.
- **Dishonesty** → recruitment reference checking to filter at hiring stage.

## Responsibility for fraud prevention and detection

- It is the **responsibility of the directors** (the board) to take such steps as are reasonably open to them to prevent and detect fraud.
- The **external auditor's** responsibility is only to express an opinion on the financial statements; audit procedures should have a reasonable expectation of detecting misstatements arising from fraud, but external auditors are not responsible for preventing fraud.
- The **audit committee** reviews the organisation's performance in fraud prevention and reports to the board.

In relation to fraud prevention, the external auditor's objective is **a reasonable prospect of detecting irregular statements or records** — NOT identifying every error.

## Fraud response plan

A **fraud response plan** is a strategy for **investigating and dealing with the consequences of suspected or identified fraud**. It is NOT a plan for preventing fraud (that is the fraud prevention strategy).

Typical contents:
- Immediate steps to secure records and evidence.
- Suspension of suspected staff.
- Investigation procedures.
- Internal and external communication.
- Crisis management.
- Recovery and prosecution.

Fraud awareness training and recruitment controls belong to the **fraud prevention plan**, not the response plan.

## Stakeholders most directly affected by fraud

When a business OVERSTATES its financial position, the most directly affected stakeholders are **investors and suppliers** — they make decisions (investment, credit) based on inaccurate information.

## Money laundering

**Money laundering** = financial transactions whose purpose is to conceal the origins of the proceeds of criminal activity. Globalisation has created more opportunities for money laundering, which governments and international bodies combat through legislation (e.g. FATF — Financial Action Task Force).

**Three phases of money laundering**:
1. **Placement** — initial disposal of the proceeds of criminal activity into apparently legitimate business activity or property. May involve banking small amounts at multiple banks to avoid reporting thresholds ("smurfing").
2. **Layering** — transfers from business to business or place to place to disguise the original source.
3. **Integration** — funds re-enter the legitimate economy as apparently clean money.

**Money laundering offences**:
- Concealing the proceeds of criminal activity.
- **Failure to report** suspicion of money laundering when required.
- **Tipping off** — informing the client (or anyone) that a Suspicious Activity Report has been made or is contemplated; this prejudices investigation.
- Assisting another to retain the benefit of criminal conduct.

**Dealing in price-affected securities** is **insider dealing**, NOT money laundering.

A professional accountant who suspects money laundering at a client must report to the firm's MLRO (Money Laundering Reporting Officer). They must NOT inform the client, as that would constitute tipping off.

Where supporting documentation is missing for cash transactions and the auditor decides NOT to take any action, the auditor has committed **failure to report**.

## Internal control — the control environment (Turnbull)

Aspects of a strong control environment include:
- Strategies for dealing with significant identified risks.
- Clear definition of authority, responsibility and accountability.
- Senior management commitment to competence and integrity.
- Effective communication of policies and standards.
- Strong ethical tone from the top.

**Control procedures** are detailed policies and procedures for administrative and accounting control (sit BELOW the control environment).
