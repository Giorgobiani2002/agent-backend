# ACCA F1 / FAB — Accountant in Business: Part D — Leading and Managing Individuals and Teams

Source: BPP ACCA Approved Practice & Revision Kit, FAB / ACCA Paper F1, 5th edition January 2016.

## Schools of management thought

- **Scientific management (F W Taylor)** — management is the development of a true science of work. Key techniques: micro-design of jobs (breaking work into single simple tasks); work-study to establish efficient methods; financial incentives. Maximising efficiency in production. Approach to job design: job simplification / micro-division of labour. Does NOT use multi-skilled teamworking. Modern applications include high-volume call centres that reorganise work into highly specific repetitive tasks.
- **Classical administrative school (Henri Fayol)** — five functions of management: Planning, Organising, Commanding, Co-ordinating, Controlling. Universal principles applicable to any organisation.
- **Empirical school (Peter Drucker)** — manager of a business has one basic function: **economic performance**. Five categories of management work (set objectives, organise, motivate/communicate, measure, develop people).
- **Human relations school (Elton Mayo, Hawthorne Studies)** — individual and group behaviour at work is a major factor in productivity. Awareness of group dynamics and worker attitudes.
- **Needs / motivation theorists (Maslow, Herzberg, McGregor)** — within the broader human relations tradition; focus on "higher-order" needs.
- **Contingency school** — no one best way; organisational design depends on a number of interacting factors (objectives, environment, staff, culture, management style, tasks, structure, technology).
- **Mintzberg** — describes managerial **roles**, arguing management is disjointed and non-systematic, not a tidy five-function process.

## Managerial vs leadership roles

Per **Warren Bennis**, key differences:
- Manager **administers and maintains**; leader **innovates**.
- Manager focuses on **systems and structure**; leader focuses on **people**.
- Manager **does things right**; leader **does the right thing**.
- Manager has a short-range view; leader has a long-range view.

## Mechanistic vs organic systems (Burns & Stalker)

**Mechanistic** (stable environment):
- Hierarchical structure of control in an impersonal organisation.
- Insistence on loyalty to the organisation.
- Specialised, narrowly defined tasks.
- Vertical communication.
- Decisions made at the top.

**Organic** (changing environment):
- Network structure of control, authority and communication.
- Lateral rather than vertical communication.
- Job descriptions less precise; harder to "pass the buck"; managers responsible for co-ordinating tasks.
- Knowledge and expertise widely spread; specialists co-operate.
- Commitment to the task rather than to the structure.

## Supervisors

A **supervisor** sits at the interface between the operating core (non-managerial workers) and management. Supervisors:
- Filter information up and down between management and operations.
- Do both operational/technical work and lower-level managerial work — they do NOT spend all their time on managerial activities.
- Manage team performance and organise the work of others.

Operational-level work is routine processing of transactions. Tactical-level work involves deciding resource allocation (e.g. how many staff needed for a project). Strategic-level work concerns future markets, new products, direction-setting.

## Recruitment and selection

- **Recruitment** is the systematic process of (a) identifying and defining skill needs, and (b) attracting suitably skilled candidates.
- **Selection** is the process of choosing the most suitable candidate from those attracted.

**First stage in recruitment**: identify the vacancy. Then write a job description, then a person specification, then advertise.

**Recruitment activities**: writing job description, designing application forms, placing job adverts.
**Selection activities**: screening application forms, interviewing, testing, reference checking.

**Typical recruitment & selection sequence**:
1. Preparing job description.
2. Preparing person specification.
3. Selection interviewing.
4. Selection testing.
5. Reference checking (only for short-listed/preferred candidates).

## Job description and person specification

- **Job analysis** — finding out the task, skill and knowledge requirements of a job. Produces:
- **Job description** — statement of key duties, tasks, objectives, reporting responsibilities and standards of the job. Used in job evaluation, training needs analysis, recruitment, interviewing. Disadvantage: can be narrow and restrictive, limiting employee flexibility and multi-skilling.
- **Person specification** — reworking of the job description in terms of the kind of person needed.

**Rodger's Seven Point Plan** (person specification headings):
1. Physical make-up.
2. Attainments (qualifications, experience).
3. General intelligence.
4. Special aptitudes.
5. Interests.
6. Disposition (temperament).
7. Circumstances.

(Note: motivation is NOT explicitly one of Rodger's seven points; it is in Munro Fraser's Five Point Pattern of Personality.)

**Competences** are capacities that lead to behaviours meeting job demands within the parameters of the organisational environment. A **competence framework** is used primarily to identify present level of competence (which then drives training needs analysis and evaluation).

## Job advertisement

A good job advertisement:
- Narrows the pool to candidates who fit (avoids irrelevant applications).
- Provides all information needed to apply.
- Honest and realistic, to manage applicant expectations.
- Targeted to the right audience via the right medium.

For a junior office assistant in a financial consultancy: a **local newspaper** is the most suitable external medium (audience targeting, cost). National newspapers are too expensive; accountancy journals reach the wrong audience.

A **policy of internal promotion** vs external recruitment:
- Negatively affects **innovation** (no "new blood").
- Positively supports **succession planning** and **induction times** (shorter — internal promotees already know the culture, systems, people).

When would the organisation be better off carrying out its own recruitment instead of using consultants? When it has time to brief them properly and complex selection criteria. When there is urgent need and consultants don't know the organisation, in-house recruitment is safer. Use external consultants when seeking innovation, new perspectives, or to handle high-volume recruitment beyond internal capacity.

## Selection interviews

Aims of selection interview:
- Compare the applicant against job requirements.
- Get information about the applicant.
- Give the applicant information about the job and organisation.
- Treat the applicant fairly.

"Getting as much information as possible" is NOT a primary aim — quality and relevance matter more.

**Question types**:
- **Open** — invites self-expression ("Tell me about...").
- **Closed** — one-word/either-or answer.
- **Leading** — pushes for a particular reply, biasing the answer ("Surely you'd agree that...?").
- **Probing** — pins down a vague candidate, especially used in combination with closed questions when the candidate seems reluctant.
- **Problem-solving / situational** — presents a scenario.

**Biases and errors in interviewing**:
- **Halo effect** — initial impression colours later perceptions. (Mistakenly applied as "first impressions" tendency.)
- **Stereotyping** — assigning candidates to a group and attributing traits of that group.
- **Contagious bias** — interviewer's body language inadvertently influences candidate's answers.
- **Projection** — attributing one's own attitudes, feelings or beliefs to the candidate.

**Panel or selection board interviews** allow multiple interviewers to see the candidate at once, reduce individual bias, and let specialists ask technical questions. Disadvantages: less rapport, questions can feel random as different interviewers shift topic, intimidating for candidate.

**Assessment centres** are NOT places — they are a series of tests, interviews and group activities conducted over several days, used to assess candidates (especially for higher-level positions) in depth.

## Selection tests

- **Intelligence tests** measure general intellectual ability.
- **Personality tests** identify character traits and behavioural preferences.
- **Aptitude tests** measure potential to develop skills.
- **Psychometric tests** include intelligence, aptitude and personality tests — they focus on innate ability or psychological traits.
- **Proficiency / competence tests** focus on current ability to perform specific tasks. Best for assessing depth of knowledge and applied ability.
- **Work sampling** — has the highest **predictive validity** of any selection method (best predictor of job performance), followed by cognitive selection tests, assessment centres, personality testing. Interviews have low predictive validity, similar to reference checking.

Criticisms of selection tests (some valid, some not):
- Practice and coaching can influence results — valid concern for intelligence/aptitude tests.
- Subjects can falsify results, particularly on self-report personality tests.
- Tests do not completely eliminate bias.
- Tests are MORE accurate predictors than interviews — so the criticism that tests are LESS accurate than interviews is NOT justified.

## Reference checking

- Two **employer** references are desirable for factual information and comparison.
- **Personal references** are selected to be supportive and have limited value.
- References provide subjective rather than objective information.

## Equal opportunities — business case

Sound business arguments for an equal opportunities policy:
- Common decency and fairness (ethics).
- Widens the recruitment pool.
- Attracts and retains the best people for the job.
- Improves the organisation's image as a good employer.

## Groups, teams and group development

- A **group** = a collection of individuals who perceive themselves as a group, with a sense of **identity** distinguishing them from non-members. Identity (acknowledged boundaries of "us" vs "them") is what distinguishes a group from a random crowd. (Random crowds may share a purpose temporarily — e.g. five pop fans — but they are not a group.)
- A group within an organisation can be **formal** (assigned by management, with defined tasks — e.g. finance department, project team, football team, orchestra) or **informal** (voluntary membership, open and informal communication, tasks NOT assigned by management).

**Tuckman's group development stages**:
1. **Forming** — tentative introduction, getting to know one another, defining the task. Briefing of current situation and asking for suggestions to move forward characterises this stage.
2. **Storming** — conflict and challenge of objectives and procedures. Factions emerge; dominating individuals clash. Innovative ideas appear; enthusiasm is high.
3. **Norming** — settling down; group agrees norms, roles, work methods.
4. **Performing** — executing the task; developmental issues no longer hinder work. Team briefings deal with progress.
5. **Dorming** — established teams may grow complacent and devote more attention to relationship/power issues.
6. **Adjourning / mourning** — wind-up at the end of a project, with renegotiation of aims and roles for new task.

When new members join an established team, the cycle restarts at **forming**.

## Belbin's team roles

Belbin identified nine complementary roles for an effective team. A healthy team has a **mix and balance** of roles — members can adopt more than one role, or switch roles. It is the process roles (how people behave and interact) that most impact functioning, not just functional/technical roles.

- **Plant** — creative ideas person; "thinks outside the box"; looks at big picture, not details.
- **Resource Investigator** — entrepreneur, networker.
- **Co-ordinator / Chair** — calm, self-confident, controlled; co-ordinates and operates through others; clarifies goals, delegates.
- **Shaper** — dynamic, outgoing, thrives on pressure; challenges and pushes team past difficulties; can annoy/upset others; committed to the task.
- **Monitor-Evaluator** — strategic, discerning critic; sees all options before judging; can be overly critical and uninspiring. Encourages team to consider alternatives, spots gaps in Plant's suggestions.
- **Team worker** — concerned with relationships; supportive; diffuses conflict; diplomatic relationship-builder.
- **Implementer** — reliable, takes ideas and turns them into actions; can be inflexible and resistant to change.
- **Completer-Finisher** — keen eye for detail; checks documents; meets deadlines; can hold up progress by refusing to delegate or sign off until all concerns are discussed; orderly, conscientious, anxious.
- **Specialist** — single-minded, dedicated; deep specific knowledge; contributes only on areas of expertise; lacks broader view.

## Team building tools and signs

**Key tools of team building**:
- Members identify with the team.
- Members commit to shared objectives.
- Diverse mix of personalities (NOT homogeneity — diversity gives a mix of roles).
- Regular reviews of performance.

**Signs of an INEFFECTIVE group**:
- Passive acceptance of work decisions (not actively engaged).
- Members do not challenge or criticise (suggests group-think or fear).
- Individuals achieving only their own targets at expense of team.

**Signs of an EFFECTIVE group** (do NOT include passive acceptance):
- Internal disagreement and constructive criticism.
- Competition with other groups (healthy solidarity).
- Active interest in decisions.

**Healthy team solidarity** is encouraged by:
- Competition with other groups (NOT within).
- Encouraging members to express disagreements.
- Avoiding group-think.

## Personality, attitudes and roles

- **Personality traits** — relatively stable enduring qualities (e.g. impulsiveness). Very difficult or impossible to change. In dealing with personality clashes in a team, do NOT try to change personality.
- **Attitudes** — mental states (thoughts, feelings, intentions) which influence an individual's response to objects and situations. Can change over time.
- **Perceptions** — how the brain selects and organises information to make sense of it. People behave according to what they perceive.
- **Role theory** — people behave according to others' expectations of how they should behave in that situation.

Practical options for managing personality conflicts:
- Educate members about personality differences.
- Remove one of the members from the team.
- (NOT — try to change their personality, which is impractical.)

## Team types

- **Multi-disciplinary (multi-functional) team** — members have different specialisms that they pool.
- **Multi-skilled team** — all members can perform any of the group's tasks (cross-trained). Manager can share tasks based on availability.
- **Self-managed team** — given discretion to plan its own task sharing and work methods.
- **Virtual team** — geographically dispersed, connected by ICT.

Teamworking is best suited to:
- Ideas generation and innovation.
- Co-ordination of different functions.
- Co-ordination of geographically dispersed workers.

Teamworking is LESS suited to:
- **Crisis decision-making** (slower; decisions may protect group at expense of right tough call).
- Highly individual creative work.

## Group communication interventions

Behaviour analysis of group contributions:
- **Proposing** — putting forward suggestions.
- **Building** — extending proposals from others.
- **Supporting** — affirming.
- **Bringing-in** — inviting contribution from others.
- **Blocking / difficulty-stating** — putting obstacles in the way of a proposal without offering alternatives. Sometimes constructive (against risky ideas), sometimes destructive (e.g. "Let's move on, we're getting nowhere" mid-brainstorming = blocking).
- **Shutting-out** — interrupting, talking over, taking over.
- **Testing understanding** — checking comprehension.
- **Summarising** — drawing together passages of discussion.

If team is bogged down in hostile argument, the leader should DISCOURAGE blocking and ENCOURAGE bringing-in, summarising and testing understanding.

## Motivation theory

**Definition (Huczynski & Buchanan)**: motivation is "a decision-making process through which the individual chooses desired outcomes and sets in motion the behaviour appropriate to acquiring them".

### Content theories — focus on WHAT motivates

**Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs**:
1. Physiological needs (food, water).
2. Safety needs (security, freedom from threat).
3. Social / love / belonging needs (affiliation, friendship).
4. Esteem needs (recognition, status, respect from others).
5. Self-actualisation (realising personal potential). The final need to be satisfied, and Maslow argues it can rarely be fully satisfied.

(Maslow also overarched the model with "freedom of inquiry and expression" needs but this is not a hierarchy level itself. McClelland's "need for affiliation" is from a different model.)

Examples by level:
- Esteem needs satisfied by: a departmental prize for performance; praise from work team for an achievement; recognition. Self-actualisation by professional development opportunity. Social needs by being invited to join colleagues for lunch.

**Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory**:
- **Hygiene factors** (also called maintenance, dissatisfiers, environmental factors) — relate to the **environment** of work. Inadequacy causes dissatisfaction; adequacy prevents dissatisfaction but does NOT motivate. Include: company policies, salary/pay, working conditions, considerate supervision, job security, interpersonal relations.
- **Motivator factors** (satisfiers) — relate to the **work itself**. Create satisfaction and motivation to superior performance. Include: achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, advancement, growth, challenging work, training and development.
- Pay is a **hygiene factor** — inadequate pay is a powerful dissatisfier. Pay can motivate short-term but according to Herzberg "a reward once given becomes a right".

Job enrichment thinking (vertical loading of responsibility, freedom, autonomy) is consistent with Herzberg.

### Process theories — focus on HOW motivation occurs

**Vroom's Expectancy Theory**:
- **Force of motivation (F) = Valence (V) × Expectancy (E)**.
- **Valence** = strength of an individual's preference for a given outcome.
- **Expectancy** = strength of belief that a given action will lead to the desired outcome.
- If V = 0 (don't care about outcome) OR E = 0 (don't believe action leads to outcome), then motivation = 0.
- Example: a salesperson sure he can win a competition (E high) but ambivalent about the team-leader prize because of family hours (V ≈ 0) → motivation low.

**Adams' Equity Theory** — people compare their input/output ratio to others'; perceived inequity demotivates.

### McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y

These are **extreme sets of managerial assumptions about workers**, NOT types of managers or types of employees. Managers' approach lies somewhere on a continuum between them.
- **Theory X** — people dislike work and responsibility; have to be coerced and controlled. Leads to tight supervision, clocking-on, rules for everything.
- **Theory Y** — people can be motivated to accept challenge and responsibility; want to be empowered. Leads to delegation, participation.

### Application of process theories

To apply process theories in practice:
- Clarify intended results.
- Give feedback on actual results.
- Provide consistency of reward for results.
- (Immediacy of reward is NOT essential — people accept delayed gratification if confident the reward will accrue.)

## Job design and reward

- **Job rotation** — moving people through different jobs at the same level (horizontal).
- **Job enlargement (horizontal loading)** — adding more tasks at the same skill level/responsibility (e.g. packer also applies date stamp, cellophane wrap and sticker).
- **Job enrichment (vertical loading)** — adding higher-level responsibility, autonomy, decision-making (giving employees freedom over how the job is done; participation in planning).
- **Micro-division of labour (job simplification)** — breaking jobs into smallest possible components (Taylorism / scientific management).
- **Division of labour** — specialisation (less extreme than micro-division).
- **Empowerment** — devolving authority to lower levels.

**Job enrichment benefits**: increases job satisfaction, enhances quality, reduces supervisory costs. Does NOT replace monetary rewards.

**Five core job dimensions** (Hackman & Oldham) contributing to job satisfaction: skill variety, task identity, task significance, **autonomy**, feedback. Autonomy is a degree of freedom/discretion — removing restrictive controls.

**Job evaluation** measures the relative value of jobs based primarily on **job content** (responsibility, complexity, working conditions, qualifications required) — NOT pay levels or market rates directly. Equal pay law requires equal pay for work of equal value, established by job evaluation.

## Rewards

- **Intrinsic rewards** — arise from the performance of the work itself (sense of achievement, interest, autonomy).
- **Extrinsic rewards** — separate from the job, dependent on others (salary, bonus, company car, working conditions, fringe benefits).
- **Incentives** — promised or offered rewards (e.g. promise of time off for meeting target).

Examples:
- Sensible company policies, considerate supervision, good salaries → all hygiene factors.
- Training programmes → motivator factor.
- Challenging work, recognition → motivator factors.

## Performance-related pay (PRP)

Potential problems with individual PRP:
- Effect on **team motivation** — team members may compete for individual reward rather than co-operate.
- Difficulty measuring less tangible criteria (teamwork, creativity).
- May not relate to total wage/salary grade structure (can create dual reward systems).

Benefits: links reward to performance, motivates short-term, supports communication of objectives, useful when employee has reached top of salary range.

## Training and development

- **Training** = "the planned and systematic modification of behaviour through learning events, programmes and instruction which enable individuals to achieve the level of knowledge, skills and competence to carry out their work effectively."
- **Development** = "the growth or realisation of a person's ability and potential through the provision of learning and educational experiences."
- **Education** = knowledge acquired gradually by learning or instruction. Forms part of training and development.
- **Conditioning** is a specific behaviourist learning technique, NOT the same as training.

A **learning organisation** (Senge):
- Generation and transfer of knowledge.
- Support for learning and development by all members.
- Willingness to continuously transform itself.
- Tolerance for risk and learning from errors (NOT minimising risk by avoiding experiments).
- "Learning climate", "reward flexibility", "enabling structures" are characteristics.

## Training needs analysis

Documents forming part of a formal training needs analysis:
- Competence standards for the job/department.
- Performance appraisal reports.
- Health & safety officer reports of failure.
- Critical incident analyses.
- (NOT — an email reminder about new legislation; that's an event prompting training need, not part of the formal analysis.)

A gap between required and current **competence** indicates a training need.
A gap between required and current **performance** may NOT be a training problem — it could be supervision, systems, motivation, resources etc.

## Learning theory and styles

**David Kolb's experiential learning cycle** — a "learning by doing" approach:
1. **Concrete experience** (have an experience).
2. **Observation and reflection** (reflect on the experience).
3. **Abstract conceptualisation** (draw conclusions/principles).
4. **Active experimentation** (plan the next steps; apply principles).

The cycle is iterative.

**Cognitive psychology** argues we use feedback information on past behaviour to make rational decisions about whether to maintain or modify behaviours according to outcomes. Reflective self-development fits this school.

**Behaviourist psychology** focuses on stimulus-response and conditioning through reinforcement.

**Honey and Mumford's learning styles**:
- **Activist** — flexible, optimistic; learns by doing, enjoys practical participation; no patience with theory.
- **Reflector** — careful, cautious, slow; learns by observing and thinking; produces carefully thought-out conclusions; works at own pace.
- **Theorist** — wants to understand basic principles; "hands-off" intellectual; prefers structured programmes run by teachers; logical argument.
- **Pragmatist** — only learns if linked to a real practical problem; likes new techniques via on-the-job training; tends to discard good ideas needing further development.

## On-the-job vs off-the-job training

**On-the-job methods**:
- Coaching (one-to-one with experienced employee).
- Job rotation.
- Temporary promotion.
- Mentoring by a colleague.
- Demonstration.
- Secondments.

**Off-the-job methods**:
- Formal college courses.
- Day release.
- Self-study courses.
- Computer-based training (CBT).
- Workshops, conferences.

**Arguments for ON-the-job approach**:
- Relevance to actual work context (informal customs and practices).
- **Application of learned skills to the job** (no transfer-of-learning problem).
- Lower cost.
- Continuity of work effort.

**Arguments for OFF-the-job approach**:
- Lower **risk of errors** during learning.
- Concentration on the learning process without work distractions.
- Specialist instructors.
- Networking across roles/organisations.

A new payroll system → trade-offs apply. For risk reduction, off-the-job is safer. For transfer of learning, on-the-job is better.

## Evaluating training (Hamblin's five levels)

1. **Reactions** — how trainees feel about the training. Inexact measure. Likely focus of trainers themselves.
2. **Learning** — what trainees know / can do after vs before. Likely focus of trainers.
3. **Job behaviour** — whether learning has been applied to the job. Focus of departmental managers (e.g. evaluating productivity gains).
4. **Organisational** — impacts on measurable performance gains for the organisation. Focus of senior management cost-benefit analysis.
5. **Ultimate value** — impacts on wider goals (social responsibility, sustainability). Often not reached.

## Quantifiable training benefits

Quantifiable: increased speed, increased accuracy, reduced errors, reduced absenteeism, reduced labour turnover.
NOT readily quantifiable: increased employee satisfaction.

## Personal development

- **Personal development planning (PDP)** — process whereby an individual defines objectives and formulates action plans for learning to improve effectiveness. Self-directed.
- **Management development** — focused on management/leadership skills (e.g. MBA).
- **Career development** — broader, anticipating future roles and lateral/vertical moves.
- **Professional development (CPD)** — maintaining/enhancing professional standards through continuing education throughout career lifetime.

When considering a personal development programme for an employee, the first stage is **setting up a competence framework** to compare against. Then identify gaps, establish learning targets, plan the programme.

## Induction

**Induction** = the process whereby a person is formally introduced and integrated into an organisation or system.

## Mentoring, coaching, counselling, appraising

Distinct relationships:
- **Coaching** — a trainee is put under the guidance of an experienced employee who shows them how to perform tasks. Job-specific skills; usually short-term; often the immediate superior. A style of management.
- **Mentoring** — a longer-term relationship in which a more experienced person encourages an individual's personal and career development. Broader than coaching. Usually NOT the immediate superior, to allow open confidential discussion. Provides a role model.
- **Counselling** — an interpersonal interview to help another person identify and work through a problem (often personal or emotional). Non-directive role: helps the person define and explore their own issues. NOT advisory or task-related.
- **Appraisal** — an interview to review performance and identify training and development needs.

In counselling:
- **Empathy** — skill of seeing the issue from the other's point of view and reflecting that understanding back.
- **Active listening** — questioning, reflecting, summarising, showing attentive body language.
- **Rapport-building** — making the person comfortable.

Counselling is essentially a **non-directive** role.

A mentor's benefits: career and personal development, providing a role model. NOT determining remuneration, deciding promotions, resolving formal grievances, or demonstrating how to perform (that's coaching).

A coach is **unlikely to** help with the trainee's personal appraisal preparation — that's a manager / mentor role.

## Performance appraisal

**Purposes of appraisal**:
- Discuss employee performance.
- Identify training and development needs.
- Reward attainment.
- Identify potential (promotion / succession planning).
- Measure performance.

NOT a purpose of appraisal:
- Discipline (handled through separate disciplinary procedures).
- Grievance handling (handled through separate grievance procedures).
- Agreeing salary (link to pay is contentious; salary review may follow but should not BE the appraisal).
- Job evaluation (that values the job, not the holder).

## Appraisal techniques

- **Overall assessment** — narrative comment without guidance.
- **Guided assessment** — assessor comments on listed characteristics and performance elements with notes on how to interpret terms.
- **Grading / rating scale** — scores against performance levels (e.g. 1=excellent to 4=poor).
- **Behavioural incident method** — compares specific behaviours against typical behaviours in each job.
- **Results-oriented scheme** — sets specific targets/standards and measures against them (e.g. sales calls made, leads, value of sales).

## Appraisal sources

- **Self appraisal** — biased by self-perception but a useful input.
- **Peer appraisal** — biased by team dynamics.
- **Upward appraisal** — subordinates rate managers; popular in progressive organisations; managers gain feedback. Problems: fear of reprisal, vindictiveness, employee point-scoring, lack of authority of reviewers.
- **360-degree (multi-source) feedback** — gathers from individual, superiors, subordinates, peers, customers. Most objective approach.

In 360-degree appraisal of a sales account manager, an **accounts payable clerk** would be least likely to provide feedback (no interaction). Credit sales clerks and direct customers would.

## Maier's three approaches to appraisal interviewing

- **Tell and sell** — manager assesses, then gains acceptance and commitment to improvement plan. One-sided.
- **Tell and listen** — manager feeds back assessment (good and bad), then invites the appraisee to discuss, ask questions, suggest targets, explain shortcomings. Encourages input from the appraisee.
- **Problem solving** — manager does not criticise; discusses issues with the employee; encourages employee to think things through and commit to personal improvement. Collaborative coaching approach.

## Performance measures — principles

Good performance measures should be:
- Related to actual key tasks of the job.
- Within the control of the individual.
- Observable or measurable.
- Challenging but achievable.
- (NOT — "easily" achievable; measures should be motivational.)

## Barriers to effective appraisal (Lockett)

- **Appraisal as confrontation** — defensive, adversarial.
- **Appraisal as judgement** — one-way assessment, no dialogue.
- **Appraisal as chat** — friendly but vague; no commitments.
- **Appraisal as bureaucracy** — form-filling for HR compliance.
- **Appraisal as unfinished business** — wrapping up unresolved issues of past year rather than focusing on future improvement.
- **Appraisal as annual event** — when on-going performance management is required.

Appraisal as an OPPORTUNITY to raise workplace problems and issues is NOT a barrier — it's positive (assuming constructive use).

## Performance management

**Performance management** = "a process to establish a shared understanding about what is to be achieved, and an approach to managing and developing people in order to achieve it." Broader than appraisal — collaborative objective-setting, on-going development and periodic review.

## Criteria for evaluating an appraisal scheme

- **Serious intent** by management.
- **Fairness**.
- **Efficiency**.
- **Co-operation**.
- **Results**.
- Relevance.
- Standardisation.
- Time allowed for preparation and training.

A double-edged sword: an **implied link between assessment and reward** can motivate seriousness, but can also threaten appraisees if reward expectations cannot be met. There are other reasons salary may not align with appraisal (budget, range maximums).

**Bonuses awarded** is NOT a suitable criterion for evaluating an appraisal system — funds may not be available.

## Conflict management

Management responses to conflict:
- **Denial** — ignore in hope it will go away.
- **Compromise** — bargain, negotiate, conciliate. Even positive compromises result in half-satisfied needs.
- **Dominance** — apply power/influence to force a settlement. Can damage relationships (win-lose).
- **Integration / collaboration** — emphasis on the task; individual esteem subordinate to group needs; jointly find win-win.

**Conflict resolution outcomes**:
- Win-win (rare but best).
- Win-lose (damaged relationships).
- Lose-lose (possible — e.g. mutual sabotage).
- Compromise (half-satisfied parties).

**Positive (constructive) conflict** can: focus attention on individual contributions; force open discussion of issues; stimulate creativity.

## Stress and time management

Time management aids:
- Assertive style of communication (saying "no" appropriately).
- Setting aside uncontactable time blocks.
- Prioritising tasks by **business importance**, not by who is shouting loudest, who is absent, or what feels urgent.
- Personal work patterns (high and low energy times).

Problems for time management:
- Open-door policies (constant interruptions).
- Sociable work groups.
- Meetings (especially without clear objectives).
- The telephone.

For a task with multiple competing demands (e.g. a public holiday with absentees, overdue reports, new staff to train), the right approach is to **prioritise in relation to the most important business outcomes**, not by pressure from the boss or by who is shouting loudest.

## Conditions for an important task

A task is important to an organisation when:
- It will satisfy a key customer.
- It will add value to the organisation.
- It is mission-critical or compliance-critical.
- The potential consequences of failure are long-term, difficult to reverse, far-reaching and costly.

The fact that an email is marked "urgent" or that the task has been delegated by superiors does NOT alone make it important.

## Negotiation techniques

- Define the problem carefully.
- Look for a wide variety of possible solutions.
- Try to develop options that result in mutual gain.
- Separate people from the problem.

Creating a trusting supportive atmosphere and evaluating progress against objectives are general team-effectiveness practices rather than specific negotiation techniques.
