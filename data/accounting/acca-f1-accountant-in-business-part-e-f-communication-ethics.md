# ACCA F1 / FAB — Accountant in Business: Parts E & F — Personal Effectiveness, Communication and Professional Ethics

Source: BPP ACCA Approved Practice & Revision Kit, FAB / ACCA Paper F1, 5th edition January 2016.

## Communication

**Communication** is a two-way process involving the transmission/exchange of information and the provision of feedback. Necessary to direct and co-ordinate activities.

The **radio signal / Shannon-Weaver model** sequence:
1. **Sender** has an idea or message.
2. **Coded message** — sender encodes the message in language/format.
3. **Medium / channel** — message transmitted via chosen medium.
4. **Receiver** — receives the message.
5. **Decoded message** — receiver decodes the message.
6. **Feedback** — receiver responds back to sender.

Correct order: Sender → Coded message → (Medium) → Decoded message → Receiver → Feedback.

In a two-way communication, **both parties** need to encode messages and both have to decode them.

## Direction of communication flows

- **Vertical communication**:
  - **Downward** — from superior to subordinate (instructions, briefings, policies).
  - **Upward** — from subordinate to superior (reports, suggestions, grievances).
- **Lateral (horizontal) communication** — between people of equal rank, e.g. heads of department who report to the same divisional manager; two directors at the same level discussing a project team's work.
- **Diagonal** — across departments and levels, e.g. a supervisor in Sales requesting help from the HR Director on a disciplinary matter (different department AND different level).

## Communication patterns (Leavitt)

- **Wheel** — central figure acts as a hub; all messages route via the centre. **Fastest** for problem-solving and reaching all recipients.
- **Y** — message goes from person to person up a chain until it reaches someone in contact with multiple people. Second fastest.
- **Chain** — message passed person to person. **Slowest**.
- **Circle** — each person communicates only with neighbours.
- **All-channel** — everyone communicates with everyone; democratic but can be slow.

Example: Charles receives information and forwards to Barry, Dilip and Elaine; Barry then informs Anna — this is the **Y** pattern.

## Information sources

- **Internal sources** — payroll system (personnel info), accounting records (sales values), minutes of meetings (decisions taken), production reports.
- **External sources** — market research, customer surveys, suppliers' catalogues, competitor websites, government statistics, industry publications.

## Formal vs informal communication

**Formal communication** desirable qualities: clarity, appropriate recipient, appropriate medium, timely.

A notice sent 2 days before a meeting scheduled 6 weeks earlier lacks **timing** — should have been sent earlier.

**Informal communication / the grapevine**:
- Operates inside the place of work.
- Fast, selective, up-to-date.
- Not necessarily accurate.
- Information is NOT divulged randomly.
- Can bypass secretive management.
- Fills a gap created by an ineffective formal communication system.
- Most active when the formal communication network is also active.
- Relates to internal politics.

## Encouraging upward communication

- **Suggestion schemes**.
- **Management By Walking Around (MBWA)**.
- **Team meetings** (genuinely two-way).
- **Open-door policies**.
- **Upward appraisal**.
- (NOT — exception reporting, which is a way of reducing manager overload but does not actively encourage upward flow.)

**Downward communication** mechanisms: notice boards, organisation manuals, team briefings, policy documents.

## Barriers to communication

- **Noise** — interferences in the environment that affect clarity, accuracy or arrival of the message.
- **Distortion** — meaning lost in coding/decoding (e.g. ambiguous language).
- **Redundancy** — using multiple communication channels so if one fails, another carries the message (positive).
- **Feedback** — confirms whether the message was correctly received (positive).
- **Misunderstanding** — e.g. slang used in task instructions, IT jargon in a wide-audience email, acronyms not explained.
- **Information overload** — too much volume or complexity for the receiver to process.
- **Status differential, cultural differences, language barriers**.

To reduce **information overload on managers**:
- **Reporting by exception** (only deviations from plan are reported).
- Filtering and summarising.
- Better delegation.
- (Using email may INCREASE overload; regular staff meetings can add load.)

## Non-verbal communication

- Includes body language, tone of voice, silences, facial expression.
- People pay **more attention to non-verbal cues** than to spoken words when interpreting meaning — this means inconsistencies between verbal and non-verbal signals undermine the message.
- Non-verbal cues are a key source of feedback in face-to-face conversation.

## Communication methods — suitability

- **Conversation** — little planning needed; gives real impression of feelings; less structured (can range widely). NOT ideal for complex ideas — those need written form or a presentation.
- **Email** — fast, supports multiple recipients, versatile (attachments). Disadvantages: lack of privacy (forwarding); people may not check regularly; requires computer literacy; complex ideas/images may not transmit well.
- **Reports** — comprehensive written record; ideal for findings to be communicated to the board.
- **Postal service** — needed when physical signatures or original documents are required (e.g. legal documents).
- **Video conference** — visual meeting for geographically distant people; preserves non-verbal cues; reduces travel.
- **Telephone** — fast for two-way conversation; lacks visual non-verbal cues.
- **Notice boards / intranet** — downward communication of static information.
- **Meetings** — multi-way; need clear objectives and an agenda.

For a sales rep visiting customers, **mobile communications** (phone, mobile data, ideally with a laptop) most directly enhance effectiveness.

## Avoiding misunderstanding in vocabulary and style

In presentations and written communications, avoid:
- **Jargon** (unless audience understands it).
- **Colloquialisms** (informal slang).
- **Double meanings**.
- (Acronyms can be used if explained first.)

Use short, simple sentences and clear vocabulary.

## Meetings

For meeting effectiveness:
- **Agree goals in advance**.
- **Prepare a focused agenda** and ensure people stick to it.
- Name the person responsible for each action point and assign completion dates.
- Include actions and dates in the minutes.

**Agenda order** (typical formal meeting):
1. Apologies for absence.
2. Approval of minutes of previous meeting.
3. Matters arising.
4. Subjects for discussion (main business).
5. Any other business.
6. Date of next meeting.

A meeting without a clear objective is NOT automatically valuable for attendees — it wastes time.

## Work planning sequence

A general work planning approach:
1. Establish priorities with management.
2. Delegate routine tasks.
3. Identify information sources.
4. Sequence interviews / data-gathering.
5. Collate information.
6. Write report.

For visualisation:
- **Bar charts** — best for showing the time required for different activities and quantitative relationships between activities (e.g. Gantt charts in project planning).
- **Network diagrams (e.g. PERT)** — best for showing sequences and dependencies.

## Project work

A **project** is an undertaking that has a defined beginning and end and is carried out to meet established goals within cost, schedule and quality objectives. Distinct from routine work.

A **contingency plan** is a plan for what should be done if changes or problems occur. A **strategy** is a long-term plan relating to organisational direction.

## Grievance, discipline and equal opportunities

A **grievance** occurs when an individual thinks they have been wrongly treated by a colleague or supervisor. Handled through a separate formal grievance procedure, NOT through appraisal.

## SMART objectives — checklist

- **S**pecific.
- **M**easurable.
- **A**ttainable / achievable.
- **R**elevant.
- **T**ime-bounded.

The "M" stands for **Measurable** (so you know when you've reached the goal). NOT manageable or motivational.

## Personal development — SWOT for individuals

When concerned about how others see you, focus on internal factors — **Strengths and Weaknesses** (these are internal). Opportunities and threats are external environmental factors.

## Failed communications and responsibilities

Example: ABC promised customer DEF a delivery date and delivered nearly a month late; DEF missed an important deadline → ABC has **failed to meet deadlines**. This is a delivery/contract failure.

## Counselling skills

In counselling:
- **Empathy** — seeing the issue from the other's view and reflecting understanding.
- **Active listening** — full attention, questioning, summarising.
- **Rapport-building** — putting the other at ease.
- **Non-directive** approach — facilitating the person to define and explore their own issues.

The aim is to help the other identify and work through problems themselves.

# Part F — Professional Ethics in Accounting and Business

## Fundamental duty of management

Managers have a **fiduciary responsibility** (duty of faithful service) to the entities whose purposes they serve.

Organisations are not autonomous; they exist to serve some **external purpose**, usually manifested in a group such as shareholders in a company or trustees of a charity. The strategic apex must not lose sight of this accountability.

## Ethical theories

- **Teleological / consequentialist ethics** — judges actions by their **consequences / outcomes**.
  - **Utilitarianism** — the right action produces the greatest good for the greatest number.
  - **Egoism** — focus on self-interest as the right outcome.
- **Deontological / non-consequentialist ethics** — actions judged by reference to **duty** and absolute moral principles (categorical imperatives). What is intrinsically right.
- **Virtue ethics** — pursuing positive moral qualities (fairness, loyalty, honesty); virtues flow into behaviour and decisions. Managers should incorporate these into decision-making.
- **Cognitivism** — there are objective moral facts.
- **Ethical relativism** — right and wrong are culturally determined; what is ethical in a given situation depends on the conditions at the time.
- **Absolutism** — there is an unchanging set of ethical principles that always applies regardless of situation.

## Criticisms of ethical positions

**Criticisms of relativism**:
- Takes no account of evolving norms (only context).
- Leads to a "philosophy of anything goes".
- Makes short-term desires equivalent to longer-term interests.
- Subject to all ethical situations is the self.
- The statement "all statements are relative" is itself an absolute (non-relative) statement — a fundamental contradiction.
- Consensus may not be possible.

**Criticisms of absolutism**:
- Takes no account of changing norms in society.
- Whatever source is used, human interpretation will lead to different views — universal agreement is impossible.

## Compliance vs integrity approaches

- **Compliance-based approach** to ethics — highlights conformity with the law and rules. The legal minimum.
- **Integrity-based approach** — wider remit; combines concern for the law with an emphasis on managerial responsibility, embedded in values and culture.

The law is a "floor" — the **minimum acceptable standard**. Non-legal regulations (industry codes, organisational rules) set higher expectations. Society's ethical expectations set still higher standards.

## Personal qualities expected of accountants

- **Reliability**.
- **Responsibility**.
- **Timeliness**.
- **Courtesy**.
- **Respect**.

## Professional qualities expected of accountants

- **Independence** — both of mind (free from actual partiality) and in appearance (avoiding situations that could cause a reasonable observer to question objectivity).
- **Scepticism**.
- **Accountability**.
- **Social responsibility**.

"Ambition" is a personal quality but is NOT considered an essential professional/ethical quality.

## IFAC / IESBA and ACCA Code of Ethics — Fundamental Principles

The IFAC code is administered by the **IESBA** (International Ethics Standards Board for Accountants). The ACCA code of ethics is based on the IFAC code.

The **five fundamental principles**:
1. **Integrity** — honest, fair, truthful; not associated with misleading information.
2. **Objectivity** — not allowing bias, conflict of interest or undue influence to override professional judgement. A complete and precise view, with negative aspects not concealed and positive aspects not accentuated.
3. **Professional competence and due care** — maintain knowledge and skill; act diligently to relevant standards.
4. **Confidentiality** — do not use or disclose information acquired in the course of work without proper authority or unless there is a legal/professional right or duty to disclose.
5. **Professional behaviour** — comply with relevant laws and avoid conduct that discredits the profession.

"Leadership", "truthfulness", "accountability" by themselves are NOT in the list of fundamental principles.

## Profession vs occupation

Characteristics distinguishing a profession from an occupation:
- **Acting in the public interest**.
- **Ethical codes of conduct**.
- **Governance by an association** (professional body).
- **Process of certification** (qualification by examination, continued membership).

(Highly skilled / training requirement / qualification by examination / highly valued services apply to both professions and many occupations.)

## Conflicts of interest — threats to objectivity

Five categories of threat (per the conceptual framework):

1. **Self-interest threat** — financial or other interest may influence judgement or behaviour.
   - A significant fee dependency on one client.
   - An audit firm where almost 20% of revenue comes from one client.
   - An ACCA member married to a director of the client (close personal/financial ties).
   - An ACCA member in direct competition with a client on a contract.
   - An ACCA member part of a joint venture with a competitor of the client.

2. **Self-review threat** — re-evaluating one's own previous judgement.
   - External auditor also helps prepare the client's financial statements.
   - Provision of taxation services as well as audit services.

3. **Advocacy threat** — promoting a client's position to the point that objectivity is compromised.
   - Offering legal services to defend an audit client being sued.

4. **Familiarity threat** — long or close relationships causing too much sympathy.
   - Audit partner has been on the audit team for the past 15 years with good relationships with senior management.
   - The financial controller of an audit client is the daughter of the audit manager.

5. **Intimidation threat** — actual or perceived pressure (including threats of litigation) deterring objectivity.
   - **Litigation** against the auditor by an audit client.
   - Client threatens to sue if the audit report is qualified again.
   - Client withholding fees to strengthen position in fee negotiation.

An ACCA member who is **an officer of the client company but does not deal with the client's affairs** is generally NOT a conflict of interest in itself (depends on safeguards).

A prospective audit firm quoting a **significantly lower fee** for assurance work than the current auditors — "lowballing" — raises a self-interest threat (especially if firm hopes to recoup via non-audit services).

## Gifts and hospitality

A firm or member of an assurance team should NOT accept gifts or hospitality unless the value is **clearly insignificant**. Regular corporate hospitality at major sporting events would normally exceed insignificance — a self-interest threat.

## Confidentiality

A professional accountant who runs into a client outside work and is asked for technical advice on the spot (e.g. inheritance tax) raises the **due care** issue — answering off the cuff risks incomplete, inaccurate, out-of-date advice. The accountant should take time to research properly.

A request from a third party (e.g. estate agent) for client information raises **confidentiality** — must seek client authority, verify identity of requester, and use secure transmission. Fax and unencrypted email are insecure.

If accounts must be printed and circulated, putting them in envelopes marked "confidential" is appropriate — NOT skipping this control to save costs. Leaving sensitive management accounts on desks breaches confidentiality.

## Whistle-blowing

**Whistle-blowing** is the disclosure to authorities of suspected illegal or unethical conduct in one's organisation. An employee in the sales tax section who notices that the employer has been understating quarterly amounts to the authorities and reports it to the tax authority is whistle-blowing.

Whistle-blowing legislation provides some protection from confidentiality breach claims.

## Bribery, grease money and corruption

- **Bribery** = payment for benefits the company is NOT legally entitled to ("bending the rules").
- **Grease money / facilitation payment** = payment for benefits the company IS legally entitled to but trying to speed up ("oiling the wheels").
- Both are unethical and in many jurisdictions illegal — regardless of the legitimacy of the underlying claim or the purpose to which the payment is put (e.g. "staff welfare fund").
- **Extortion** = payment under threat.
- Bribery is sometimes legal — meaning the statement "bribery can sometimes be legal" is FALSE. Bribery is generally unethical and illegal.

## Ethical issues for manufacturers

Ethical issues for a manufacturer of fast-moving consumer goods include:
- **Materials used** (safety, environmental impact, eco-friendliness).
- **Product quality** (safety, customer satisfaction).
- **Advertising** (truthfulness, manipulation, vulnerable audiences).
- **Supplier labour practices** (ethical sourcing) — even though indirect, reputational damage is significant.
- **Packaging** (safety, eco-friendliness, truthful labelling).

## Ethical employment practices

Ethical objectives for employment practices:
- Guarantee of minimum wages / living wage.
- Proactive health, safety and welfare promotion.
- Promotion of workforce diversity.
- Fair recruitment and selection.

"Employability training" helps employees secure other employment — NOT in itself an ethical objective in the same sense.

## Corporate governance — concepts

In the context of corporate governance and ethics:
- **Transparency** — open and clear disclosure of all relevant information. Example: providing financial projections to shareholders that were used as the basis for a major investment decision.
- **Integrity** — adhering to principles of professionalism even under pressure. Example: refusing to authorise a misleading advertising campaign despite peer pressure.
- **Responsibility** — accepting accountability for one's decisions and their consequences. Example: a director accepting blame for an unsuccessful training programme he introduced.
- **Judgement** — making decisions that enhance the prosperity of the organisation. Example: declining a takeover that would only have boosted directors' status without adding shareholder value.
- **Fairness** — taking into account everyone with a legitimate interest in the company; respecting their rights and views.
- **Openness** — clear disclosure.
- **Probity** — telling the truth and not misleading stakeholders.
- **Accountability** — answerable for the consequences of actions.
- **Independence** — not under any financial or other obligation to outside individuals/organisations that might influence official duties (Nolan Committee on Standards in Public Life definition of integrity).

## Public expectations and the public interest

To ensure that the policies of an organisation are consistent with the public interest, directors should focus on **the collective well-being of stakeholders** — a wide group including shareholders, employees, customers, suppliers, the community.

There is a public expectation that accountants will act ethically. Professional ethics should be followed even at the expense of contractual obligations.

## ACCA students and code of ethics

ACCA students and members **must always follow ACCA's code of conduct**. The code applies in all professional contexts.

## Corporate codes of ethics

A corporate code of ethics typically includes:
- Professional ethics requirements.
- Organisational culture and values.
- Standards of behaviour and conduct expected of employees.
- Stakeholder relationships.

A corporate code of ethics is NOT designed around **organisational self-interest** — that would defeat its purpose.

When designing a code of ethics, the most important consideration is the **minimum acceptable standards of behaviour and conduct of employees**. Legal requirements alone do not eliminate unethical behaviour. Specific customer complaints may not be representative. Sector norms may not be ethical.

## Frameworks vs detailed rules

**Framework-based approach** to ethical codes:
- Encourages proactive discussion of issues.
- Suits complex situations and evolving environments.
- Encourages professional development.
- (Does NOT guarantee consistent application — there is high discretion in applying guidelines.)

**Rules-based approach** prescribes detailed rules but can be inflexible and gameable.

# Cross-cutting key facts and definitions

## Time value of money, inflation effects

- During **high price inflation**, the value of money reduces over time.
  - **Debtors / borrowers** gain — repay less in real terms.
  - **Creditors / lenders** lose — receive less in real terms.
  - An organisation with many **long-term payables** benefits from inflation.
  - An **exporter** to a low-inflation country faces challenges as the domestic price rises faster than what foreign customers will pay.
  - A **large retailer with high inventory and low turnover** loses because inventory was bought at older (lower nominal) prices but holding costs increase.

## Equilibrium in markets

- **Equilibrium price** — where supply equals demand.
- A **minimum price below equilibrium** — no effect (market clears above the minimum).
- A **minimum price above equilibrium** (price floor) — creates excess supply (surplus).
- A **maximum price above equilibrium** — no effect.
- A **maximum price below equilibrium** (price ceiling, like rent controls) — creates excess demand (shortage).

When **rent controls are eased** after a homelessness crisis: rents rise, supply of rented housing rises (in the longer term, building activity increases), the gap between demand and supply narrows, homelessness decreases. Easing rent controls is unlikely to substantially affect owner-occupied dwellings.

## Demand and supply shifts — worked examples

- **Fall in price of coffee** → quantity of coffee demanded RISES (movement along the curve). Tea (substitute) demand falls; price of tea may fall. Drinking cups (complement) demand rises slightly.
- **Sharp rise in price of cut flowers** → demand for flower vases (complement) falls → vase demand curve shifts LEFT → vase price falls.
- **Rise in price of hovercraft tickets** → demand for sea ferry tickets (substitute) increases → ferry demand curve shifts RIGHT → ferry price rises and more ferry tickets are sold.
- **Fall in sterling** → London hotels become cheaper for foreign tourists → demand for hotel rooms rises.
- **Fall in aeroplane ticket prices** → travel cheaper → demand for hotel rooms rises.
- **Fall in US dollar value** → US tourists find London more expensive → demand for hotel rooms falls. (So if the question asks what would REDUCE demand for London hotels, the answer is a fall in the value of the US dollar.)

## Price elasticity calculation — point method

If price falls from $20 to $15 (a 25% decrease) and quantity rises from 10 to 15 (a 50% increase):
- PED = 50% / 25% = 2 (elastic).

Note signs are usually ignored when stating PED in absolute terms.

## Effective demand in the economy

Aggregate demand = Consumer spending (Consumption) + Investment + Government expenditure + Net exports (gains from international trade).

## Macroeconomic policy combinations

To **expand** the economy:
- Reduce taxation (more disposable income).
- Increase government expenditure.
- Reduce interest rates (cheaper borrowing, more investment and consumption).
- Increase money supply.

To **slow** the economy:
- Increase taxation.
- Reduce government expenditure.
- Raise interest rates.
- Reduce money supply.

A combination of **increased government spending + reduced taxation** stimulates demand AND raises the Public Sector Net Cash Requirement (PSNCR) — more borrowing.

## Ageing population and demographics

An **ageing population** results from **falling birth rate + falling death rate** (equal rates of change). Other combinations:
- Rising birth + falling death → younger and growing.
- Rising birth + rising death → roughly stable age structure.
- Falling birth + rising death → ageing AND shrinking.

Demographic trends like falling birth rates have implications for an ageing workforce, skill shortages, and HR planning across most organisations.

## Government direct influence on the economic structure (Porter)

Government affects industry via:
- **Demand** (as a major customer).
- **Capacity expansion** (e.g. via investment tax incentives).
- **Competition** (via regulations and antitrust).

## Decentralisation — pros and cons

**Advantages**: greater awareness of local conditions, fewer bureaucratic obstacles, better motivation of junior managers, more accountability at lower levels, better crisis management, avoids overburdening top managers.

**Disadvantages**: increased risk (less central control), inconsistency of decisions across the organisation, duplication of effort.

## Internal vs connected vs external stakeholders

- **Internal**: management, employees, directors (people inside the organisation).
- **Connected**: shareholders, customers, suppliers, financiers, partners (contractual relationships).
- **External**: government, regulators, pressure groups, local community, general public, professional bodies.

A finance director and an HR director of the same company are both **internal** stakeholders.

Customers and suppliers are **connected** stakeholders. Local residents in a planning dispute are **external** stakeholders. Trustees of a charity who hold land on its behalf and are required to sign land contracts are **connected** stakeholders (contractual fiduciary role).

## Power culture as Greek god

Charles Handy associated his cultural types with Greek gods:
- **Power culture — Zeus**.
- **Role culture — Apollo**.
- **Task culture — Athena**.
- **Person / existential culture — Dionysus**.

## Best practice in ethics codes — safeguard

The most effective safeguard for ensuring high ethical standards (e.g. honest competitive conduct, no deception of competitors) is **effective education and training for all staff** — instilling standards into the culture. Fines, external review of policy and rewards for whistle-blowing are weaker safeguards on their own.

## Why stakeholders care about ethics

Reasons stakeholders care about an organisation's ethical conduct:
- **Customers** — purchases may be influenced by ethical considerations (ethical consumerism).
- **Employees** — need to know what is expected of them, and want to work for an organisation they can be proud of.
- **Local communities** — need reassurance the organisation will act as a "good citizen".
- **Investors** — ethical lapses cause reputational and financial damage.
- **Suppliers** — relationship quality and payment ethics matter.

A coach is unlikely to help a trainee prepare for an upcoming appraisal — that's a mentoring or management activity, not coaching of technical skills.
